What is guaraná

Guaraná is a climbing vine from the Amazon basin whose seeds are the basis of Brazil’s best-loved sparkling soft drink. This page covers what the fruit is, where it comes from, its history, and how it becomes a drink.

The plant

Guaraná as a plant

Guaraná (Paullinia cupana) is a climbing plant in the soapberry family, Sapindaceae. It grows in the Amazon basin and is especially common in Brazil, both in the wild and under cultivation.

The plant has large leaves and flowers borne in clusters. Its stem climbs through neighbouring vegetation and can reach considerable length — guaraná is a vine rather than a tree, though in cultivation it is often trained into a shrub.

The fruit ripens to a colour between brown and red. When it splits open, it reveals a black seed partly wrapped in a white aril. The result — a black seed in a white casing framed by red skin — looks so distinctive that the comparison to an eye has become practically unavoidable, and appears in almost every description of the plant.

The plant’s name comes from the language of the Sateré-Maué people, from the word warana, which passed through Portuguese into most other languages.

Botanically, guaraná sits in the same family as lychee and longan — a slightly surprising relationship for a plant most people associate with caffeine rather than tropical fruit.

Origin

The Amazon and the Sateré-Maué people

Guaraná is deeply bound up with the culture of the indigenous peoples of the Amazon. The Sateré-Maué of Brazil have cultivated and used the plant for centuries, long before Europeans arrived.

In the Sateré-Maué origin myth, a deity sows the plant’s seeds in two places — one variety in the forest and one in the village. The myth accounts for both the wild and the domesticated form of guaraná at once, which is a remarkably precise observation about the relationship between people and plant.

The traditional preparation is also known to the Guaraní people, who make a herbal drink called cupana. The method deserves attention, because it is strikingly resourceful. The seeds are shelled, washed and dried, then pounded into a fine consistency. That is kneaded into a dough and shaped into cylinders, which are then dried — producing what is usually called guaraná bread. The cylinders keep for a long time, and a small quantity is grated off when needed and steeped in hot water with sugar.

The practical advantage is obvious: in a climate where fresh produce spoils quickly, guaraná becomes a shelf-stable form that travels well and can be used in small portions.

The plant became known to Europeans in the 16th century through Spanish and Portuguese chroniclers, among them Felip Betendorf. Guaraná gradually spread beyond the Amazon, but Brazil remains its principal producer to this day.

Composition

Caffeine in the seed — and why the drink is a different matter

Guaraná seeds are among the most caffeine-rich plant materials in nature. By weight they contain roughly 6% caffeine, while green coffee beans contain between 1% and 3%. That ratio is why guaraná comes up so often whenever caffeine is discussed.

Caffeine content of the raw material, by weight of the dried seed or bean
Raw material Caffeine (% by weight)
Guaraná seeds about 6%
Green coffee beans 1 – 3%

There is an important qualification here that often gets skipped. The high caffeine figure applies to the raw seed, not to the sparkling drink. A soft drink such as Guaraná Antarctica uses extract at a low concentration — enough for flavour and character, but far from the concentration found in the seed itself.

The result is that a sparkling guaraná drink contains appreciably less caffeine than an ordinary cola of the same volume. This is a soft drink made for refreshment, not a product built around a stimulant effect.

Composition figures follow the standard botanical descriptions of Paullinia cupana. The exact caffeine content of any given drink is always stated on its label.

From fruit to glass

How guaraná becomes a drink

Guaraná is found in a number of forms, but the most authentic and by far the most widely recognised is the Brazilian sparkling drink. In Brazil it is not an exotic item but an everyday one — it sits on the table alongside the other staple soft drinks and holds a clear place in the country’s food culture.

The process runs broadly as follows: the seeds are harvested and dried, and an extract is drawn from them. That extract is combined with carbonated water, sugar and the remaining ingredients to produce the finished drink. The result surprises people who expect something close to coffee or cola — guaraná is mild, lightly fruity and considerably less sharp than citrus sodas.

The most common description of the flavour is “mild and slightly apple-like, with a berry after-flavour”. That mildness explains why the drink works equally well on its own, with food, and as a base for non-alcoholic cocktails.

Today

Guaraná in the present day

Brazil remains the world’s principal producer of guaraná. The plant is grown in the Amazon, and part of the harvest still comes from communities for which guaraná is a traditional crop rather than simply an agricultural commodity.

Within Brazil, guaraná is one of the everyday sources of caffeine — not as a concentrate, but through the soft drinks that occupy the same place colas hold elsewhere. That matters for understanding the product: in a Brazilian context guaraná is a drink for the table and for company, not something consumed for an effect.

Outside Brazil, guaraná is also produced in neighbouring Paraguay, and Brazilian brands are exported to dozens of countries. It is that export trade which makes the authentic drink available in Bulgaria today.

The brand

Guaraná Antarctica — the best-known guaraná drink

Created in 1921, Guaraná Antarctica is the most recognisable guaraná drink in the world and one of Brazil’s best-loved soft drinks.

It is produced in five countries today, and bottled for the European market in Portugal by Sumol+Compal. It is this European version that LA Associates imports into Bulgaria.

Questions

Frequently asked questions about guaraná

Is guaraná a fruit or a plant?

Both. Guaraná is the name of the climbing plant Paullinia cupana and of its fruit — more precisely the seeds, which are what is actually used.

Where does guaraná come from?

From the Amazon basin, chiefly in Brazil, which remains its principal producer today.

What does the guaraná fruit look like?

The fruit is coloured between brown and red. When it splits, it reveals a black seed partly wrapped in a white aril — a look often compared to an eye.

What does guaraná taste like as a drink?

Mild, lightly sweet and fruity in character, most often described as close to apple with a berry after-flavour. Considerably less sharp than citrus sodas, and quite distinct from cola.

Is there a lot of caffeine in a guaraná drink?

No. The raw seed is caffeine-rich, but the sparkling drink uses extract at a low concentration, so the finished product contains appreciably less caffeine than an ordinary cola of the same volume.

What is the best-known guaraná drink?

Guaraná Antarctica, created in Brazil in 1921 and known worldwide today.
